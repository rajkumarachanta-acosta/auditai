## 2026-08-16 — Topic: Keyword bloat dilutes budget (structure quality myth-bust)

**Angle tag:** `keyword-bloat`, `structure-quality`, `myth-bust`

**Hook:** "More keywords does not mean more sales. On most accounts I audit, it means the opposite."

**Post:**

More keywords does not mean more sales. On most accounts I audit, it means the opposite.

I pulled the numbers across dozens of Amazon ad audits this year. The pattern is remarkably consistent: accounts running 300+ active keywords per campaign convert worse, not better, than accounts running 30.

Here's the myth: more keywords = more coverage = more sales. Here's the mechanic actually happening: Amazon's algorithm splits impression share across every active keyword in a campaign. Add keyword #301 and you're not adding incremental reach — you're diluting the budget and signal that keywords #1 through #50 were using to actually convert.

The accounts with 30 tight keywords aren't missing traffic. They're winning the auction more often on the terms that matter, because the campaign isn't fighting itself for the same budget pool.

The fix isn't "add more." It's "prune to what's proven, then expand deliberately, one high-conviction term at a time — and watch what happens to the keywords you already had."

If your agency's monthly report celebrates "keywords added" as a metric of progress, ask instead: what's our conversion rate per active keyword, and is it going up or down as that number grows?

More isn't coverage. More is often just noise competing with your own budget.

## 2026-08-09 — Topic: ASIN portfolio triage as capital allocation (not ad optimization)

**Angle tag:** `asin-portfolio`, `capital-allocation`, `framework`

**Hook:** "You don't have an ad problem. You have a capital-allocation problem wearing an ad problem's costume."

**Post:**

You don't have an ad problem. You have a capital-allocation problem wearing an ad problem's costume.

Every Amazon account I've audited has the same three buckets hiding in the data, and almost nobody looks at ad spend through them.

Cash Cows — the ~20% of ASINs already producing 80% of revenue. Spend here shouldn't be "optimized" for ACOS. It should be pushed until the marginal dollar stops returning. Cutting these campaigns to hit an ACOS target is trading margin for a metric.

Need Love — high revenue-per-view, low total revenue. Demand-proven products starving for exposure. This is the highest-ROI place to add budget, and it's almost always underfunded because nobody's watching for it.

Reduce/Pause — spend with no revenue signal and no efficiency signal. This is the money everyone finds eventually, months too late.

Three buckets. Most Amazon ad budgets are still allocated flat across all three, as if a dollar in each bucket is worth the same. It isn't.

Before you approve next month's ad budget, ask your team one question: "Show me spend by bucket, not by campaign name." The campaign name tells you nothing about where the dollar is going. The bucket tells you everything.

## 2026-08-02 — Topic: Q4 prep — inventory/ad-spend sync (timing risk)

**Angle tag:** `q4-prep`, `inventory-ad-sync`, `story`

**Hook:** "Every Q4 disaster I've audited started in October. The mistake was actually made in August."

**Post:**

Every Q4 disaster I've audited started in October. The mistake was actually made in August.

Two accounts, same category, same ad budget, same Prime Big Deal Days:

Brand A treated ads and inventory as separate workstreams. Ops ordered stock off a sales forecast. Marketing set budgets off a media plan. Nobody synced the two until the campaigns were already live and outbidding demand they couldn't fulfill.

Brand B did one thing differently in August: they mapped ad spend velocity to inventory coverage, ASIN by ASIN, before a single bid went up. When a hero SKU had 6 weeks of stock left and Q4 needed 10, they throttled that campaign in September — not December, when the buy box already showed "temporarily out of stock" and organic rank started sliding.

Brand A's CFO got a Q4 that looked like a win on the ad dashboard and a loss on the P&L: high spend, high ACOS on the SKUs that stocked out, and a rank hit that bled into January.

If your ad plan and your inventory plan live in separate spreadsheets right now, that's not an ops problem. It's a Q4 revenue problem, and August is the last month to fix it while it's still cheap.

## 2026-08-02 — Topic: ACOS vs. contribution margin (metric blind spots)

**Angle tag:** `metric-blindspot`, `founder-questions`, `contrarian`

**Hook:** "Your ACOS is 18%. Your agency is thrilled. Your CFO shouldn't be."

**Post:**

Your ACOS is 18%. Your agency is thrilled. Your CFO shouldn't be.

Most Amazon sellers optimize the wrong number.

ACOS measures ad efficiency. It says nothing about whether the business is actually making money.

I've audited enough accounts to see the same gap every time: a campaign report showing 18% ACOS sitting on top of a P&L showing a 30%+ return rate, thin margins, and a handful of ASINs carrying 80% of revenue while ad budget gets spread evenly across all of them.

None of that shows up in the metric your agency reports on.

Three questions worth asking before your next QBR:

1. What % of ad spend touched a keyword that generated zero sales this month? Not "low ACOS" — zero.
2. What's blended TACOS — total ad spend over total revenue — not just attributed ACOS?
3. Which 20% of ASINs are absorbing 80% of the ad budget, and is that where the margin actually is?

A campaign can hit every ACOS target and still bleed cash if the wrong products are getting funded or returns are quietly eating the sale.

Ad efficiency is a tactic. Contribution margin is the outcome. Founders who only see the first number are flying with half the instrument panel.
